● Cross-cutting topic 2019—2026 technologies of managed competition

Spoilers as an industry.

Spoiler parties in Russia's political landscape are not a side-effect of weak regulation — they are a systematically deployed infrastructure for managing elections. The technology operates on three layers: onomastic (decoy / namesake candidates via legal name changes at the civil registry), ideological (technical parties with a similar brand or niche agenda), and technological (DEG (remote electronic voting), consolidation of polling stations). All three layers are documented in open sources; none violates current legislation — which is itself the key fact. Canonical cases: Boris Vishnevsky (Вишневский) in the St Petersburg Legislative Assembly election 2021 (three 'Boris Vishnevskys' on one electoral commission poster), Rashkin in the Moscow City Duma 2019, and the Communist Party of the Russian Federation (Коммунисты России, CPKR) deploying namesake spoilers against the CPRF in 7 of 15 Moscow single-member districts in the 2021 State Duma election.

A · Onomastic spoilers

Decoy / namesake candidates: legal name changes as a technology

The canonical case of onomastic spoilery is Boris Lazarevich Vishnevsky (Борис Лазаревич Вишневский), a deputy of the St Petersburg Legislative Assembly since 2011, standing for Yabloko in a single-member constituency at the 2021 St Petersburg Legislative Assembly election. By early August 2021 Vishnevsky was the most recognisable Yabloko figure in St Petersburg — a public critic of the city administration, a columnist in Novaya Gazeta, and the author of books on St Petersburg politics. In August 2021, two self-nominated candidates with the identical full name registered in his constituency.

The first — Boris Ivanovich Vishnevsky: before changing his name at the civil registry — Viktor Bykov (Виктор Быков), a State Counsellor of St Petersburg, standing for the Green Party; he grew a beard, and his campaign posters were a visual copy of the real Vishnevsky's photographic style. After the election he reverted to his original name. The second — Boris Gennadyevich Vishnevsky: before the change — Alexei Shmelev (Алексей Шмелёв), head of sales at LLC Euro-Project Group; he likewise grew a beard, and his campaign materials shared the same visual style as the genuine Vishnevsky's. On the composite poster issued by the Central District of St Petersburg's election commission all three candidates were visually indistinguishable (Коммерсантъ, BBC Russian).

CEC (Russia's Central Election Commission) chair Ella Pamfilova (Элла Памфилова) described the situation at a press briefing as a 'disgrace' and the 'lowest point to which political technologists have sunk' (Коммерсантъ, 4975445). Despite the CEC chair's public reaction, neither decoy was removed from the ballot: current legislation does not prohibit changing one's name to any name. The original Vishnevsky was re-elected, but his result was diluted: instead of the projected maximum of 32% he received 26%. The structural significance of the case is that the 'lowest point' is sanctioned by the legislative framework rather than in breach of it. The technology is not outside the law — it operates within legal boundaries, and is deployed systematically.

A similar scheme was used in the Moscow City Duma election in 2019: in the Lyublinsky constituency, where Valery Fyodorovich Rashkin (Валерий Фёдорович Рашкин, CPRF) was standing, an exact namesake — another Valery Fyodorovich Rashkin — was registered. According to the Golos (Голос) election-monitoring movement, in Moscow alone in 2019 no fewer than 14 cases of namesake spoiler candidates were recorded; former CPRF official Konstantin Zhukov publicly stated on 13.07.2020 that the structures funding the decoys were linked to the mayoral administration team (single source only — reliability level 🟠).

In the 2021 State Duma election in Moscow, Communists of Russia (CPKR) fielded namesake spoilers against the CPRF in 7 of 15 single-member districts — this is no longer an isolated incident but a systematic technology, deployed against an opposition party in every key constituency.

B · Ideological spoilers

Technical parties: niche-filling without genuine agency

An ideological spoiler is a party that absorbs part of a rival's electoral niche without possessing any real political agency. Unlike the onomastic layer, the technology here is legal without qualification: the party obtains Ministry of Justice registration, has a programme and constitution, and fields candidates. The structural marker is a programme that replicates part of the agenda of a major opposition player (the CPRF, SRZP, or Yabloko), while genuine coalition activity with that player is absent. Canonical examples: four parties from different parts of the spectrum.

Communists of Russia (CPKR) is the CPRF's main spoiler party. Registered by the Ministry of Justice on 7 June 2012 — coinciding with the wave of party-registration liberalisation following Bolotnaya Square. Leader 2012–2022: Maxim Suraykin (presidential election 2018: 0.68%, approximately 498,000 votes). A clone of the CPRF in name and rhetoric ('Stalinists'); the primary mechanism — fielding namesake decoys against CPRF candidates in single-member districts, especially in Moscow. In March 2022 Suraykin was removed by 'his own' party congress, and the formal change of leadership was approved by the Ministry of Justice — meaning that even the spoiler party is controlled administratively through statutory procedures. Full dossier: Communists of Russia.

Party of Pensioners for Social Justice (RPPSJ) — a social-populist niche party at the intersection of the CPRF and SRZP electorates. Founded in 2007, rebranded several times. Result in the 2021 Duma election: 2.45% (below the 3% threshold; the party receives no state funding). Key regions: Sverdlovsk Oblast, Altai Krai. Its programmatic agenda reduces to pension indexation: the party has no ideological programme beyond that single issue and functions as a pure protest-channelling vehicle in the social segment. Full dossier: Party of Pensioners.

RPFJ (formerly CPSS, renamed March 2021) — a social-democratic niche party run by Andrei Bogdanov (Андрей Богданов). Result in the 2021 Duma election: 0.77%. In the 2024 presidential election Bogdanov withdrew in January. Structurally RPFJ is the 'third layer' of left-flank spoilers (alongside CPKR and RPPSJ): in each election cycle, depending on the tactical requirement, one of the three is deployed. Full dossier: RPFJ.

Green Alternative — the ecology niche. Founding congress: 10 March 2020 (nine days after the New People founding congress); Ministry of Justice registration: 7 April 2020 — in the same 'registration window' as New People (24.03.2020) and Prilepin's For Truth party (25.03.2020). The coincidence of registration dates for three 'new' parties within 18 months of the election is an indicator of a coordinated project wave. Result in the 2021 Duma election: 0.64%. Primary function: vote-splitting from Yabloko (whose electorate partly associates it with an environmental agenda) and the CPRF (part of whose base links it to a socialist environmental agenda). Full dossier: Green Alternative.

C · The 'Bogdanov laboratory'

One person — five or more parties across different election cycles

Andrei Vladimirovich Bogdanov (Андрей Владимирович Богданов) is a political technologist; organiser and leader of several parties active in different periods. The list includes the Democratic Party of Russia (DPR; Bogdanov led it in 2005–2008), the Party of Peace and Unity (PME; a small party active in the 2000s), the CPSS (registered in 2012 during the post-Bolotnaya registration-liberalisation wave), renamed in March 2021 as RPFJ (Russian Party of Freedom and Justice), and a number of micro-projects. In the 2008 presidential election Bogdanov himself stood as a candidate and received 1.3%; in the 2024 presidential election he withdrew in January.

The structural feature of this construction is that one person manages parties across multiple generations, each fulfilling different tactical tasks in different years. When a given election cycle calls for a liberal niche — the DPR is activated; when a 'third social-democrat' is needed — the CPSS/RPFJ is fielded; in niche constituencies — other micro-projects are deployed. This is an infrastructure of 'parties built to order', not a party as a durable political actor. For more on its structure and instruments, see the dossier RPFJ.

The term 'Bogdanov laboratory' is a functional description that has been consistently reproduced in the political-science literature and business-press publications since 2007. There is no direct documented evidence in open sources of this laboratory's connection to the Presidential Administration — which is why the term is marked at the 🔴 level (author's interpretation). Structurally the construction is self-reproducing: the parties Bogdanov runs are regularly registered with the Ministry of Justice, field candidates in every major election cycle, and in the majority of cases fail to clear the threshold for state funding — which is itself an argument in favour of the 'laboratory', rather than electoral, nature of these projects.

D · Technological spoilers

DEG, consolidation of polling stations, absence of video monitoring

Remote electronic voting (DEG) was launched in Moscow for the 2019 Moscow City Duma election, extended to the 2021 State Duma election, and was treated as the benchmark system by the 2024 presidential election. The technical infrastructure is Mos.ru (the Moscow version) and Gosuslugi (the federal version). Criticism from the Golos movement, publications by Boris Vishnevsky, and Sberbank IT specialists centres on three points: the impossibility of independent recounting; coercion into DEG by employers in the state and municipal sector (public-sector employees — 'бюджетники'); and cases of 'proxy registration' without the voter's consent. In the 2021 Moscow Duma elections the gap between paper-ballot and electronic results in some districts reached 25–30 percentage points in favour of pro-government candidates. A detailed analysis of DEG's mathematics and the overall electoral system is on the Electoral Maths page.

Consolidation of polling stations and the shift to multi-day voting are parallel technological transformations. Since 2021 there has been a systematic consolidation of UIKs (polling station commissions) (from 1,500 to 3,000+ voters per station), which complicates independent observation. The reduction in the number of UIKs in Moscow — from approximately 3,700 (2018) to approximately 3,300 (2021) alongside a growing voter roll — was recorded by Golos. Multi-day voting (initially three days, then extended) makes round-the-clock uninterrupted observation by a single observer team impossible. In the 2021 Duma election — for the first time since 2012 — there was a complete absence of publicly accessible video streaming from UIKs; cameras were running, but footage was available only to CEC observers. In the St Petersburg Legislative Assembly 2021 election, in Vishnevsky's constituencies, video monitoring was partly absent even for accredited observers.

In single-member districts a parallel technology operates: the 'agreed district'. United Russia does not field a candidate, and the district is left for a candidate from a 'coordinated party' — for example, Civil Platform (Гражданская платформа), run by Shaikhutdinov, a de facto dormant shell party that has had no independent electoral activity since Prokhorov's departure in March 2015. In the 2021 Duma election the party received 0.15% and 1 single-mandate seat (Shaikhutdinov himself). In exchange for the district — loyal voting by the single-mandate deputy along faction lines.
