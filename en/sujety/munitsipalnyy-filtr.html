The municipal filter — an architectural constraint.

The municipal filter — a Russian rule requiring gubernatorial candidates to collect signatures from 5–10% of local councillors and elected municipal heads across ¾ of all municipalities in a region — was introduced in 2012. Because United Russia controls 65–80% of local council seats in most regions, an opposition candidate cannot pass the filter without the ruling party's consent. In the period 2012–2026 not a single non-systemic governor has emerged in any major region. The mechanism works not through ballot fraud on election day but through an architectural restriction on the candidate field at the registration stage.

A · What the filter is

Federal Law No. 40-FZ of 02.05.2012 — amendments to Art. 18 of Federal Law No. 184-FZ

The municipal filter was introduced by Federal Law No. 40-FZ of 2 May 2012 — a package of amendments to Article 18 of Federal Law No. 184-FZ "On the General Principles of the Organisation of Legislative (Representative) and Executive Bodies of State Power of the Subjects of the Russian Federation" (text of FZ-40 on pravo.gov.ru). The law was passed at the ebb of the 2011–2012 street protests: the restoration of direct gubernatorial elections had been one of the demands of Bolotnaya Square, and the filter was the condition on which the authorities agreed to restore them. The practical effect — maintaining manageability while formally reinstating the electoral procedure.

The key requirements for a candidate for head of a constituent entity of the Russian Federation: from 5% to 10% of signatures from local councillors and elected heads of municipal formations (the exact percentage is set by the regional legislative assembly within that band), with signatures representing at least ¾ of the municipalities in the region — i.e., covering three-quarters of all territories. Each local councillor may support only one candidate (one councillor, one signature); signatures must be notarised. If a candidate has collected signatures in fewer municipalities than the regional law requires, registration is refused without examination of the total quantity on its merits.

The structural logic of this provision lies not in the percentage itself (5–10% is formally a modest figure) but in the combination of three constraints: (1) a narrow pool of signature-holders (only local councillors, whose party affiliation is rigidly fixed), (2) geographic coverage (¾ of municipalities — meaning signatures cannot be gathered in one or two "friendly" districts), (3) the uniqueness principle (one councillor, one signature, turning the pool into a zero-sum game between candidates). Given a municipal hierarchy in which United Russia controls 65–80% of seats, any opposition candidate is compelled to request signatures from United Russia councillors — who by default are required to clear that decision with the regional party leadership.

B · Municipal hierarchy structure

≈218,000 local council seats — who holds them

According to consolidated data from Russia's Central Election Commission (CEC) and the Ministry of Justice (2024–2025), the Russian Federation has approximately 218,000 local councillors and elected heads of municipal formations at all levels — from urban districts to rural settlements. These 218,000 people constitute the pool from which a gubernatorial candidate must collect 5–10% of signatures. In absolute terms that is between 11,000 and 22,000 signatures per region (adjusted for the region's size); in large regions it is between 200 and 500 signatures at the typical 7–8% midpoint.

The defining characteristic of this hierarchy is its party composition. Aggregate statistics from the CEC are not publicly consolidated; estimates are produced by the Golos (Голос) movement through sampling across several dozen regions and cross-referencing parties' own reports on faction membership in local assemblies. Simple arithmetic makes the impossibility plain. To pass the filter at the typical 7% midpoint, an opposition candidate in a large region needs roughly 7% of approximately 3,000–5,000 local councillors — that is, 200–350 signatures. Even if the candidate collected 100% of the signatures of every councillor from every parliamentary opposition party (CPRF + LDPR + SRZP + New People + Yabloko), the combined total comes to around 9–13% of seats — close to the lower bound of the band, but with no margin for refusals and without meeting the geographic requirement (¾ of municipalities). In practice, consolidating all opposition councillors behind a single candidate is ruled out: parties compete with each other, the CPRF and SRZP in the same region typically back different candidates, and LDPR functionaries have historically voted for approved candidates. Any realistic opposition signature drive runs into the necessity of obtaining signatures from United Russia councillors — and they issue signatures only to approved candidates.

C · Canonical cases

Roizman 2017, Levchenko 2019–2020, Moscow 2018 and 2023

Ye. Roizman — Sverdlovsk Region, 2017

Yevgeny Vadimovich Roizman (Евгений Вадимович Ройзман) — former mayor of Yekaterinburg (2013–2018), one of the most recognisable regional politicians on the liberal wing in the 2000s and 2010s, and founder of the City Without Drugs foundation. In 2017 he attempted to stand for governor of Sverdlovsk Region as the candidate of Yabloko. The regional law of Sverdlovsk Region set the municipal filter at 7.9% — meaning that in absolute terms the region required the collection of approximately 260 signatures from local councillors across 79 municipal formations (with mandatory coverage of ¾ of them).

According to publicly available campaign data, Roizman managed to collect approximately 80 signatures — close to 100% of all available opposition councillor signatures in the region, since United Russia councillors refused to co-operate and the pool of opposition councillors in the region was limited and had already been partly distributed between other approved candidates. The regional election commission refused to register him (Ведомости, 12.07.2017). This case is canonical because Roizman — a recognisable, high-rated, sitting mayor of the regional capital — collected the maximum physically accessible to the opposition and still fell short of the requirement. The impossibility here is structural, not reputational.

S. Levchenko — Irkutsk Region, 2019–2020

Sergei Georgievich Levchenko (Сергей Георгиевич Левченко) — governor of Irkutsk Region for the CPRF, elected in a second round in September 2015 with 56.4% against the then incumbent United Russia governor Sergei Yeroshchenko. This was one of the extremely rare instances of a major region passing into the control of a non-systemic — formally opposition — governor. The entire subsequent dynamic of the "Irkutsk case" illustrates how the regime dismantles such victories not at the next election through the filter, but in advance, through a combination of instruments.

Chronology of the dismantling: 2 October 2019 — Andrei Sergeyevich Levchenko (Андрей Сергеевич Левченко), the governor's son and a CPRF member of the Irkutsk regional legislative assembly, was detained in connection with an alleged embezzlement of 185 million roubles from the Irkutsk Region budget; 12 December 2019 — Levchenko senior submitted his resignation ("at his own request", under pressure from the federal centre); early gubernatorial elections were held in July 2020, at which Levchenko senior failed to pass the municipal filter and was refused registration as a candidate; the winner was Igor Ivanovich Kobzev (Игорь Иванович Кобзев) (United Russia, former deputy minister for Civil Defence) with 60.79%. 28 July 2022 — Andrei Levchenko was sentenced to 9 years in a penal colony. The Irkutsk case is the cleanest documented example of removing an elected opposition governor through a combination of instruments: a criminal case against a close family member as a pressure point, voluntary resignation as a way out, and the municipal filter as a block on electoral return.

The structural lesson — the filter operates not only against new opposition candidates but also against opposition governors who have already been elected and tested by public support: after a single exit from the system, return via elections becomes impossible. As a party, the CPRF received in 2020 in Irkutsk Region, and in every subsequent regional cycle, a "political economy lesson": winning a governorship leads not to electoral gain but to attrition through prosecutions of family members and the blocking of electoral comebacks.

Moscow — 2018 and 2023

Moscow is Russia's largest electoral entity, and the municipal filter there is set at 6%, requiring approximately 110 signatures. In the 2018 mayoral elections all four candidates admitted alongside Sergei Sobyanin — Mikhail Degtyaryov (LDPR), Ilya Sviridov (SRZP), Vadim Kumin (CPRF), and Mikhail Balakin (independent) — were "approved" candidates, meaning they could collect signatures precisely because United Russia had issued a directive to its local councillors to share signatures. Sobyanin received more than 70% of the vote. In the 2023 mayoral elections the ballot contained, in addition to Sobyanin, Leonid Zyuganov (CPRF, grandson of the party leader) — 8.11%, a candidate from the LDPR, and Dmitry Gusev (SRZP); all of them were likewise "approved". Non-systemic candidates — Alexei Navalny (prior to his death in a Federal Penitentiary Service facility in February 2024), Mikhail Svetov, Alexander Yankauskas, and others — were physically unable to pass the filter, since they had not and could not have a single supportive local councillor in Moscow; the pool of opposition municipal councillors in the capital in 2018 and 2023 consisted primarily of Yabloko and CPRF councillors, whose signatures were distributed by party leadership among the "approved" contenders.

D · The opposition paradox

Why parliamentary opposition parties do not build up their municipal base

The most puzzling fact about the system on superficial inspection is that the parliamentary "opposition" parties (CPRF, LDPR, SRZP) do not systematically build up their municipal base, even though it is precisely the absence of that base which physically blocks their own candidates from standing in gubernatorial elections. If the CPRF were to raise its share of local council seats in even a handful of targeted regions from 4–6% to 10–12%, the filter would technically cease to be impassable for it (without coalitions, without United Russia signatures). Yet from 2012 to 2026 the opposition parties have conducted no such targeted campaigns to build a municipal base.

Possible explanations for this paradox (all three at the 🔴 author's interpretation level):

Hypothesis 1 — functional. The parliamentary "opposition" parties do not need gubernatorial power in principle. Their function within the political system is to represent protest votes in the State Duma and regional legislative assemblies, not to govern constituent entities of the federation. Holding a governorship imposes real accountability on the party for the region (budget, utilities, social obligations), which interferes with its rhetorical positioning as "opposition" in the Duma. The Irkutsk case of Levchenko 2015–2020 is an instructive example of how a governorship proved for the CPRF not an electoral asset but a prolonged drain.

Hypothesis 2 — administrative pressure. Every elected local councillor from an opposition party is a potential target for audits (Federal Tax Service, Prosecutor's Office, Investigative Committee), dismissals from employment (for public-sector employees and employees of state-owned and municipal enterprises), and criminal cases (especially under Article 159 of the Criminal Code — fraud in municipal financial matters). A party that actively expands its municipal base thereby creates a growing number of such targets; the costs of maintaining the base rise faster than the electoral returns from it. It is possible that the parties are aware of this dynamic and consciously refrain from scaling up their municipal work.

Hypothesis 3 — combined. The parties could, but their leadership deliberately does not want to: the status of "parliamentary opposition" with formal presence in several regional assemblies and the Duma is a stable position that provides the party leadership with financial and media resources without requiring them to take responsibility for any regions. Any real expansion of the municipal base automatically pushes the party towards agency (nominating its own candidate for governor), which means leaving the managed system. The opposition paradox — in this reading — is not a paradox at all but a systemic equilibrium: opposition parties exist precisely because they make no claim on the regions; making such a claim transforms them into suppressed actors, as happened to Levchenko and his son.
