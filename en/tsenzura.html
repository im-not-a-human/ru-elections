Between 2021 and 2026, Russia enacted a package of digital restrictions: blocking and throttling of foreign platforms, mandatory SIM biometrics, fines for 'searching for extremist content' and 'advertising' VPNs, mandatory pre-installation of the national messenger, formalised FSB authority to demand disconnection of communications. Every measure is justified by security — counter-terrorism, anti-fraud, child protection, protection against extremism. Yet measurable indicators against those very goals have worsened: record growth in fraud and IT crime, a several-fold rise in drone attacks, the Crocus City Hall terrorist attack coordinated via Telegram. In parallel, a legal VPN 'whitelist' covering tens of thousands of IP addresses has been built for the state, banks, and large businesses, while ordinary citizens face administrative liability for searching for content and bypassing restrictions. This page is about the gap between stated goals and measurable outcomes. Every figure is linked to a primary source.

The central contradiction

Declaration and architecture.

Security features in the rhetoric and sometimes in the actual threat model. But when one looks at the design of the measures — which services remain accessible, who receives exemptions, who bears the costs, who is shielded from liability — a different pattern becomes visible.

Official rationale
We disconnect the internet to protect against drones and fraudsters
  • Blocks — to protect against extremism and terrorists.
  • Mobile internet shutdowns — against UAVs.
  • VPN ban — against criminals.
  • MAX — for convenient identification and anti-fraud.
Actual architecture
Access to selected services; bypass by application only
  • A whitelist of 500+ Russian services.
  • Corporate VPN access for 1,730 companies and 57,000 IP addresses.
  • Mandatory pre-installation of MAX and migration of banks, utilities, and schools.
  • Operators relieved of liability for disconnections demanded by the FSB.
  • Fines for citizens for 'searching' and 'advertising' VPNs.
  • Restrictions on voice calls by MAX competitors.
  • Parallel secure communications for security services (ATS-1, ATS-2, VCh — government secure telephone exchanges).

02 · VPN

Tiered-access VPN.

Roskomnadzor (Russia's federal media and telecommunications regulator) blocks mass-market VPNs as a threat — yet itself grants exemptions for technological processes and corporate access. Already in 2021 the scheme was not 'VPN is dangerous as a technology' but 'mass-market VPN is dangerous; corporate VPN is permissible'. By 2026, the selective regime had grown by an order of magnitude.

Reaction within the pro-government camp

Survey divergence

An open online poll by New People (more than 370,000 participants) found that 72% consider the law harmful. A quota-sampled poll by VTsIOM / Insoomar in August 2025 — 61% in favour. The divergence is typical: open self-selected polls tend to capture more active opinion; quota polls may register more loyal or socially desirable responses. Both figures are presented without choosing which is 'correct'.

03 · SIM cards, drones

The anti-drone logic is partly real.

Mobile SIM cards can indeed be used in certain UAVs — for telemetry transmission, final guidance, or communication with the operator. That must be acknowledged. But it does not follow that any mass blockage of mobile internet and mandatory pre-installation of MAX constitute necessary anti-drone measures. Divergent justifications. The chair of the State Duma's information-policy committee, Sergei Boyarsky (Сергей Боярский) (RIA Novosti, 13.08.2025): "foreign messengers have become the only space that cannot be influenced." One documented guidance channel for long-range UAVs is mobile communications via Russian operators within signal range, or via foreign operators roaming at the border; satellite links, radio channels, autopilot, inertial navigation, and visual navigation are used in parallel. Regulatory effort is concentrated on Russian citizens using VPNs and WhatsApp, not on the channels through which attacks are coordinated.

UAV attacks on Russian territory: 6,200 → 23,000+
A 3.7-fold increase in one year according to Security Council data (Sergei Shoigu (Сергей Шойгу), March 2026): "not a single region of Russia is protected."

04 · Whitelists

A reversal of internet logic.

Ordinary blocking works on the principle of 'everything is permitted except what is prohibited'. A whitelist works in reverse: 'everything is prohibited except what is permitted'. This is not a quantitative but an architectural shift: the default set of accessible resources changes entirely.

Growth of the whitelist: from ~12,000 to 57,000+ IPs over eighteen months
Growth of the corporate VPN exemption according to Roskomnadzor and vc.ru. Condition for inclusion on the Ministry of Digital Development's whitelist — Russian computing infrastructure.

Sources: Roskomnadzor via Interfax (22.04.2026); vc.ru; Profil; Forbes (Ministry of Digital Development). The divergence between estimates (57,000 from Roskomnadzor vs 75,000 from vc.ru) reflects different methodologies: Roskomnadzor counts addresses and subnets for specific companies; vc.ru aggregates including standard packages of licensed corporate VPNs.

Everyday test: does 'everything needed for daily life' work?

AP reported that during Moscow's mobile internet shutdowns even services on the whitelist stopped working: government services, top banks, taxi applications. ATMs, parking machines, maps, delivery services, car-sharing, and retail were also affected. Human Rights Watch (31.03.2026) documented users losing access to maps, messengers, ATMs, taxis, and card payments — and whitelists sometimes failing to work.

05 · Meta · YouTube · Discord · MAX

Forced audience migration.

Each individual decision is explained by a separate threat: Meta — extremism, YouTube — technical degradation, Discord — counter-terrorism, restriction of voice calls — anti-fraud. But taken together they constitute a single architecture: MAX's competitors are displaced, while MAX itself receives mandatory pre-installation, a budget of 4 bn ₽, and the migration of banks and utilities — in parallel with the throttling and blocking of alternatives.

Messengers: declaration ≠ fact

Sources: Mediascope (October 2025); Alfa-kurs / Mediascope (2025) for monthly reach. MAX has been mandatory for pre-installation since 01.09.2025; does not use end-to-end encryption; is integrated with the FSB, MVD, Federal Tax Service, and Central Bank. Russian Armed Forces units are prohibited from using MAX at the front (iStories, 23.02.2026).

YouTube: 'technical degradation' and political punishment

YouTube traffic index in Russia: 48.8 → 7.8 (×6.3)
Google Transparency Report. In parallel, the Netherlands index rose from 6.2 to 10.0 — due to VPN traffic landing at foreign data centres.
"The degradation of YouTube is a forced step directed not against Russian users but against the administration of a foreign resource." "It is no coincidence that the summer period was chosen to bring YouTube to its senses." — Alexander Khinshtein (Александр Хинштейн), Telegram, 25–26.07.2024
"The slowdown of YouTube in Russia is not the result of any technical problems or actions on the part of Google." — Google, via RIA Novosti, 23.12.2024

Discord: pro-war Z-channels against the block

After Discord was blocked on 08.10.2024 (audience of ~30 million in Russia at that moment), military Z-channels erupted in protest at the loss of a UAV coordination channel.

"No one expected such a stab in the back. Our lawmakers and 'ban-merchants' are just as terribly far from the iron-hard arse of reality, the people, and, regrettably, the army. Discord serves to transmit video images to headquarters at various levels." — Fighterbomber (530,000+ subscribers), via Meduza, 09.10.2024
"At the command posts of dozens of units, drone feeds operating through the messenger's private rooms went down." — Z-channel 'Troika', via Meduza, 09.10.2024

Russia's Ministry of Defence made no official comment on either the block or military use of Discord. According to a Meduza investigation of 06.02.2026, the primary 'application' of Starlink in the war zone was streaming drone footage through Discord.

06 · Failures by the stated metric

The very metrics used to justify the measures are worsening.

This is the strongest part of the evidence. All figures are drawn from official sources: the Central Bank of Russia, the MVD, the Investigative Committee, the Security Council, Sberbank. The period is 2022–2025 — precisely the years in which anti-fraud legislation, mandatory SIM biometrics, blogger and foreign-agent registers were enacted, and platform blocking was in progress.

Funds stolen from citizens' accounts: 14.2 → 29.3 bn ₽ (×2.06)
Data from the Central Bank of Russia. Sberbank (Kuznetsov, Federation Council, January 2026) estimates the aggregate annual loss at 275–295 bn ₽ — eleven times the Central Bank figure.
IT crimes: 522,000 → 765,400 (40% of all crime)
MVD of Russia. 2024 was a record year; in January–July 2025 — 424,900 cases with losses of 119.6 bn ₽ (+16% against 2024).
Child suicides: 311 (2020) → 753 (2021) → 735 (2024, +8.4% vs 2023)
Investigative Committee, MVD. After 'child protection' legislation enacted in 2022–2024, the rate has remained above 2018–2020 levels. The claim that digital restrictions resolve the problem is not empirically supported.

The Crocus City Hall terrorist attack, 22.03.2024

Despite the full legislative framework in place at the time — foreign-agent and blogger registers, the Meta block, IDO (information dissemination organiser) status for Telegram, the TSPU (deep-packet-inspection filtering infrastructure) system, the Yarovaya Law requiring mandatory traffic retention — the attack took place. According to the Investigative Committee (29.03.2024), defendants confirmed under questioning that their actions were coordinated by a man using a pseudonym through voice messages on Telegram. The Telegram channel through which payment was made (a cryptocurrency wallet, $2,525) was deleted on 01.04.2024. Verdict (January 2025): 149 killed, more than 600 wounded, 27 defendants.

07 · Selective enforcement

Four tiers of application.

Digital restrictions from 2024–2026 are formally universal. In practice they operate on at least four tiers: for senior officials a parallel infrastructure is in place; large businesses receive legal VPN; private users face the full fines regime; foreign agents are stripped of political rights.

08 · International comparison

Russia: between Turkey and Iran.

Russia is not simply 'defending itself against drones'. It is reproducing the standard toolkit of states with unfree internets: blocking, throttling, shutdowns, pressure on VPNs, national platforms, identification, permission lists, exemptions for loyal entities, legal opacity.

Freedom on the Net: 30 → 17 / 100 (new low in 2025)
Russia is identified in Freedom House 2025 among the 5 countries with the worst multi-year decline (alongside Egypt, Pakistan, Turkey, and Venezuela). According to the RSF Press Freedom Index 2025, Russia ranks 171/180.

Conclusion

Security as a legitimising frame.

The available data do not support the claim that the digital restrictions of 2021–2026 achieved their stated objectives — data from law-enforcement agencies and financial regulators record the opposite trend. But the thesis of a complete absence of effect is also incorrect: the Meta block led to redistribution of audiences and advertising budgets in favour of Russian platforms, and TSPU has become an instrument for real-time traffic filtering. The honest strong conclusion is different.

Russia's digital-restrictions policy uses genuine security risks as the basis for a disproportionately broad control infrastructure.

This infrastructure does not merely protect against threats; it transforms the very model of the internet — from an open network to a network of permitted services, permitted entities, and permitted communication channels.

What is changing in parallel with the growth of digital restrictions:

  1. The measures technically address only part of the stated threat.
  2. They systematically create permissive exemptions for state-approved entities.
  3. They legally shield operators and the state from liability to users.
  4. They systematically coincide with economic support for national platforms (43.5 bn ₽ from the Ministry of Digital Development's budget for VK and MAX in 2025).
  5. They are systematically recorded as deterioration on the very metrics used to justify them (fraud, IT crime, UAV attacks).
  6. They alter the very architecture of network access: from blocking individual sites to managing layers of access.
  7. They bring the Russian internet closer to the models of China, Iran, and Belarus.
