Methodology

How to read this report

This report analyses 18 key laws on digital and civil freedoms from 2019–2025, together with supplementary votes on social policy for context. All data are drawn from official State Duma sources, state media, and independent fact-checking publications.

Sources, caveats and limitations

Primary sources: sozd.duma.gov.ru (bill records), transcripts of plenary sessions at duma.gov.ru, roll-call results at vote.duma.gov.ru, publications from TASS, RIA Novosti, Rossiyskaya Gazeta, Parlamentskaya Gazeta, Kommersant, Vedomosti, RBC, Forbes, and Interfax, as well as party websites er.ru, kprf.ru, ldpr.ru, spravedlivo.ru, newpeople.ru. Roskomsvoboda, OVD-Info, Meduza, and Novaya Gazeta were used for context.

A key caveat on 'real opposition influence'. United Russia holds 325 of 450 seats — a constitutional majority sufficient to pass any law without a single opposition vote. Even a consolidated 'against' vote from CPRF, LDPR, A Just Russia – For Truth, and New People cannot block government initiatives. The vote is therefore transformed from an instrument of influence into an instrument of political positioning. The central question is how far even this 'cheap' positioning is actually used by the factions.

'Opposition from the right'. Some 'against' votes from CPRF and A Just Russia – For Truth represent not objections to restrictions but demands that they be made stricter. For example, Nina Ostanina (CPRF) called for criminal liability when the LGBT propaganda law was passed; CPRF proposed criminalising 'childfree propaganda'. This must be distinguished from genuine liberal opposition.

Technical limitations. Direct automated scraping of sozd.duma.gov.ru and vote.duma.gov.ru is constrained by anti-scraping measures; precise per-faction figures were therefore compiled from secondary sources. Where figures were unavailable, this is clearly noted.

About the author and editorial position

The site's author is not a political scientist or sociologist; their professional background is in an adjacent technical field (automation systems, AI agents).

This site is an experimental project: all analysis, calculations, texts, and interactive widgets were produced by AI agents under the author's supervision — which is itself part of the author's specialism. The aim of the experiment is to demonstrate that modern tools allow anyone to compile multi-layered analytical material from open-source data and subject it to scrutiny.

This is analysis of public data, not political agitation. The site contains no calls to action, is not affiliated with any political movement or organisation, and does not aim to discredit any government body. Every claim is supported by a link to its primary source; readers are free to draw their own conclusions.

If you notice a factual error, please open a pull request in the project's repository (github.com/im-not-a-human/ru-elections); the material is maintained and updated as corrections are received.

Source criteria

This site relies primarily on primary documents: the texts of laws, CEC (Russia's Central Election Commission) resolutions, Duma transcripts, and roll-call voting results. Where quotes from public commentators are cited (Schulmann, Gallyamov, Kynev, Katz, Lyubarev, and others), these are illustrations of positions, not confirmations of facts; facts are confirmed only by primary sources.

The site's author does not identify with any political camp — neither 'liberal' nor 'statist' — and does not treat the views of any individual commentator as an independent argument. Inclusion of a quote in this material does not imply the author's endorsement of the quoted person or their activities.

The central paradox

On freedoms — a united bloc.
On social policy — a real conflict.

The axis of genuine political conflict in the Duma does not run along 'freedom versus control' but along 'social-democratic course versus market course'. On internet regulation and foreign agents — five factions form a united bloc. On budgets and pensions — real disagreements emerge.

Conclusions

What the numbers show.

The central pattern

Seven years of voting reveal a paradox: the axis of genuine political conflict runs not along 'freedom versus control' but along 'social-democratic course versus market course'.

On any restriction of the internet or civil rights, unanimous support from all five factions is the expected outcome, with a margin of ±5%. On budgets and taxation — a stable split of 3 factions against 2.

The VPN law of 22 July 2025 is so far the only alarm signal for the system: for the first time in this convocation, two factions (CPRF and New People) openly voted against a digital-freedom restriction on principled grounds.

What this means for voters

Vote on social policy — there is a choice. The genuine confrontation between the right bloc (United Russia + LDPR + New People) and the left (CPRF + A Just Russia – For Truth) on taxation, budgets, and pensions is a stable, documented dynamic.

Vote on digital freedoms — there is almost no choice. Apart from minimal and episodic opposition from New People and CPRF, the parliamentary mechanism operates as a single machine for narrowing digital and information space.
